Alu audit: what was done and what was found

Tool-assisted audit of Alu subfamilies and copies in the human T2T-CHM13 assembly. Session log: background, copy set, sequential peeling, COSEG, and SINEderella step 2 with an AluY control.

updated 2026-11-11 · repository github.com/Toki-bio/ALU_audit · this page is stored there as docs/alu_audit_process.html · full detail in README sections 9–17 · compute on Monsoon

Where the project stood

The repository held starting notes only: nothing had been run. The notes list what exists in other repositories (all on hg38), what the literature leaves open, and a proposed order of work. This session picked it up with three instructions: learn the existing tools, use Monsoon for computation, and fetch the background literature, listing what could not be fetched.

Timeline

1 · Background

Tools read from source. SubFam 1.5.1, the peel (peel_features.py in SINE-discriminator), both Alu benchmarks in SubFam, the Tal whole-hg38 SINEderella run, SINE_orth_loc, MSA-viewer, and the SINEderella–COSEG test design. Findings that shape the plan:

  • SubFam orders copies with an n × n k-mer matrix: 30,000 copies need 3.6 GB, so the whole Alu set cannot go through it in one run.
  • The peel was calibrated on Timema. On Alu it has run only on 295 Konkel loci.
  • COSEG was built only in a cloud container; no server has it.
  • Not recorded anywhere found: the SINEderella command line of the Tal hg38 run, and the script behind its RepeatMasker concordance numbers.
2 · Literature

About 30 sources downloaded and every literature statement in the README checked against the opened text. Quotes and locators are in literature/SOURCES.md. Papers that were paywalled or bot-blocked were fetched by hand by the owner (two batches of ten) and added.

4 · Decisions
  • Reference call set, chosen by the owner: RepeatMasker 4.2.2 with Dfam 3.9, run on hs1 and dated 2025-10-31.
  • Step 3 of the plan, in the owner's words: “subfam on all alu is impractical, sequential peeling is the key.” Reading used here, to be confirmed: SubFam on a sample, peel the isolated groups, build each consensus from member copies, assign and remove those copies from the whole set, sample again from the remainder, repeat until only ungroupable copies are left.
  • Mode: autonomous from this point.

A copy 13–16 % from the consensus of a subfamily that expanded in the human lineage is older than that subfamily can be, so these rows are candidates for a wrong name. Possible causes to separate: an old AluS or AluJ copy matching a young consensus by chance or through parallel CpG changes; gene conversion; a subfamily that is in fact old (AluYh9 has no row under 6 %). Nothing here has been tested yet.

8 · SubFam on a sample

824,790 full-length dimeric Alu extracted (one RepeatMasker row for its ID, at least 0.9 of the consensus covered, 250–350 bp, monomers left out; 247.7 Mbp, no N). A seed-1 sample of 30,000 went through SubFam 1.5.1 -n 50: 600 chunk consensuses in 5 min 7 s, 3.6 GB. A 1,000-copy toy test passed first.

Chunk purity against RepeatMasker names: mean 0.613; 64 chunks at 0.9 or more, 143 at 0.7–0.9, 393 below 0.7. RepeatMasker names are similarity calls themselves, so this is not a verdict on the chunks.

Failures on the way. The first two jobs died in the sampling line (sort | head under pipefail: broken pipe). My first patch did not apply and I resubmitted the unfixed script without checking. The third job ran after I verified the fix on Monsoon.

The peel at its default parameters (none changed after seeing Alu data) found two groups, then reported "nothing separable".

At defaults the peel recovers the old, intermediate and young families and nothing inside AluS. Round 3 found 22 features and no block among the 360 residue chunks. The AluJ chunks sit at both ends of SubFam's ordering (ids 1–99 and 574–600).

Next, as the owner described it: take the S copies out of the whole set and sample again from that remainder.

10 · Assignment of all copies

Consensuses built from 500 member copies per group match Dfam closely: G1 is AluJo at 97.0 %, G2 is AluY at 100 %, the residue is AluSx at 99.7 %.

Two failed attempts, both mine. One ssearch36 run on the whole library reported only 62,957 of 824,790 copies. In blocks, with -z 1, the statistics were estimated from a library that is entirely Alu, so best and runner-up differed by under 5 bits for nearly every copy; the built-in control (0.669) stopped the job. I should have used SINEderella step 2 from the start.

SINEderella step 2, unchanged (10 cycles, 10/10 unanimity, 0.45 × top-N), 36 min on 32 cores: 797,760 of 824,790 copies firm (96.7 %): 527,234 to the AluS consensus, 145,702 to AluJ, 124,824 to AluY. Control passed: 27,123 of 29,019 firm round-1 sample copies (0.935) went to their own group.

11 · Peel, round 2 (AluS only)

A new sample of 30,000 firm AluS copies (seed 2) went through SubFam -n 50 (600 chunks, mean purity against RepeatMasker names 0.509) and the peel at defaults: 21 features, no block, no group.

Removing AluJ and AluY did not make structure inside AluS visible at these settings. A likely reason, not yet tested: each chunk consensus is a plurality vote over 50 copies that are mostly AluSx, so diagnostic bases of the smaller S subfamilies (AluSp, AluSc, AluSq, AluSg) are outvoted inside the chunks and never reach the peel.

12 · 10-copy chunks (owner's option 1)

Same AluS sample, SubFam -n 10 -p 0.6: 3,000 chunk consensuses. The prediction, written before the run: if small subfamilies were outvoted inside 50-copy chunks, purity must rise and the small AluS names must hold chunks of their own.

Partly confirmed. AluSp (103 chunks at 0.7 or more, against 12 at chunk size 50) and AluSc (26, against 1) separate once chunks are small. AluSx1, AluSz, AluSz6, AluSq2 and AluSg do not (1–7 chunks each): their copies are not neighbours in SubFam's whole-copy k-mer ordering even at 10 copies per chunk, so chunk size is not what hides them. The peel found 258 features, no block, no group.

13 · COSEG and RepeatMasker

COSEG (built from GitHub, commit cea334a) ran on the same 30,000 AluS copies with the settings of its Alu example; its bundled Price example ran first as a control (4 subfamilies). 28,715 copies passed Price's end-completeness rule. COSEG found 26 subfamilies, each defined by named diagnostic positions.

Overall agreement with RepeatMasker names: V-measure 0.283. COSEG separates AluSz, AluSz6 and AluSq2, which SubFam's ordering and the peel never did: it tests co-occurring changes at single positions, while SubFam orders copies by whole-copy similarity. COSEG and RepeatMasker both work from consensus positions, so their agreement is not independent proof of real lineages, and the CpG question is still open.

Defect: COSEG stopped while building consensus sequences (an insertion site in subfamily 24) and still exited 0. The partition is complete; the consensuses and tree were not written.

14 · COSEG vs RepeatMasker, judged by SINEderella step 2

The owner's question: can COSEG and SINEderella be compared directly? The test runs SINEderella's assignment step (step2_asSINEment.sh, unchanged) on all 527,234 firm AluS copies twice, with two banks:

  • COSEG bank: the 26 COSEG subfamilies. Each consensus is built from up to 500 member copies (MAFFT, then a plurality vote per column), because COSEG did not write its own consensuses.
  • RepeatMasker bank: the 17 Dfam 4.0 AluS families (AluSc … AluSz6), unchanged.

Script scripts/13_bank_vs_S.sbatch, jobs 34200646 (COSEG) and 34200647 (Dfam).

Step 2 reads as follows. Two near-identical consensuses differ only at their diagnostic positions, so the difference in score between them is the contribution of those positions. The 10 shuffled cycles with a 10/10 unanimity rule remove copies whose win is within the noise. A copy that wins all 10 cycles is firm; the rest go to soft assignment in step 3.5 and are not lost.

A correction. My first reading of the toy said that step 2 “cannot assign” AluS copies, and I proposed scoring on diagnostic positions instead. The owner objected: step 2 was built to focus on diagnostic positions through its 10 votes. The objection is right, and the reading above replaces mine.

Toy (first 2,000 copies): 44 firm with the COSEG bank (2.2 %) against 26 with the Dfam bank (1.3 %). These 2,000 copies all come from chr1:17,012–8,916,930, and the full run shows they were not representative (step 16).

15 · Control with a known answer: AluY

Why a control. The owner concluded that “the alu requires careful reconsideration because current system doesn't hold”. Before reading a low firm rate as “these subfamilies are not real”, the same step 2 was run where the answer is known. AluYa5 and AluYb8 are real lineages, confirmed by population data.

Inputs: the 124,824 firm AluY copies and all 20 Dfam AluY consensuses, step 2 unchanged (scripts/14_control_Y.sbatch, job 34200661). Result: 6,398 firm (5.1 %).

AluYa5 passes. AluYb8 fails, but only on unanimity. AluYb8 is the plurality call for 2,057 of its 2,276 copies (90 %), always with 6–9 votes of 10 and never 10. A vote-by-vote check showed where the other votes go: 2,055 of those 2,057 copies give them to AluYb9 (5,237 of 20,570 votes, against 15,286 for AluYb8). The two bank consensuses are 318 bp each and differ at one aligned position (column 174: AluYb8 C, AluYb9 G). One diagnostic base is within step 2's noise, so the cycles split between the two.

Lesson. A low firm rate cannot by itself mean that a subfamily is not real. It also measures how close the bank's consensuses are to each other. The broad parental consensus (AluY) collects almost no firm copies.

In both sets the firm rate falls as copies get older. That is what decay erasing diagnostic positions would produce. But age and subfamily are mixed: the young bins are mostly AluYa5 and AluSp. So the pattern fits decay without proving it.

A measure that does not work for Alu

SINEderella flags a firm copy as a leak when the runner-up scores at least 0.90 of the winner. Every firm copy in both runs has a ratio of 0.92 or more, most of them 0.96–0.98. So all firm copies are leaks (100 %). Alu subfamilies differ at fewer positions than the SINE subfamilies that the 0.90 cut was set for, and here the cut separates nothing. It is not used.

17 · Full run, COSEG bank, and the direct comparison

Job 34200646 ran for 3 h 03 min. With the 26 COSEG subfamilies as the bank, 66,181 of 527,234 AluS copies are firm (12.6 %), against 22.4 % with the RepeatMasker bank.

Self-check: of the 3,652 firm copies from COSEG's own input sample, 2,444 (0.669) go back to their own COSEG subfamily. The largest one, coseg_sf0 (5,661 sample copies), is 4.8 % firm and only 0.4 % back to itself, so it behaves like a parental consensus, as AluSx and AluY do. coseg_sf18 has no firm copy.

The two banks make most of their firm calls on different copies. Where both are firm, most COSEG subfamilies map onto one RepeatMasker name:

  • Clean, 89.7–100 %: sf13 → AluSc5 (2,059 of 2,059); sf23, sf15, sf24 and sf2 → AluSp; sf1 and sf17 → AluSc; sf4 → AluSz6; sf11 → AluSc (94.9 %); sf16 → AluSz6 (89.7 %).
  • Partly clean: sf9 → AluSz (76.4 %).
  • Mixed: sf19 (50 % AluSz6), sf5 (49 % AluSz), sf7 (31 % AluSz).

AluSp is the clearest case. COSEG has four subfamilies whose firm copies are AluSp (sf2, sf15, sf23, sf24). Together they hold 1,475 firm copies, against 43,598 firm to AluSp under the RepeatMasker bank. This fits the AluYb8/AluYb9 lesson: when a young lineage is split into close consensuses, its votes split and its copies stop being firm. That would also explain why young copies are rarely firm with the COSEG bank.

How far apart are the four AluSp subfamilies?

The five bank consensuses (COSEG sf2, sf15, sf23, sf24 and Dfam AluSp) were aligned with MAFFT, 313 columns. Substitutions are counted from AluSp. Gaps are left out: they come only from poly-A tail length and from the N at AluSp column 133.

consensusdifferences from AluSp
coseg_sf20. It is AluSp.
coseg_sf151: column 264, C→T, not at a CpG.
coseg_sf242: column 73, G→A, and column 224, G→C. Both are at CpG sites of AluSp.
coseg_sf238. Five are CpG transitions (columns 140, 176 and 232 G→A; columns 270 and 278 C→T). Three are not (27 A→C, 135 T→C, 288 A→G).

Pairwise, sf2 and sf15 are 1 base apart, sf2 and sf24 are 2, and sf15 and sf24 are 3. So sf2, sf15 and sf24 are near-twins of AluSp, one to three bases apart: the same situation as AluYb8 and AluYb9, where the copies split their votes and few become firm.

sf23 is further away (8–10 bases from the other three), but most of its differences are CpG transitions. It could be a real AluSp sub-lineage, or AluSp copies that share CpG decay; this check cannot tell the two apart.

So far

What the data show:

  • Young subfamilies with their own diagnostic positions are recovered firmly and agree with RepeatMasker: AluYa5, AluSp, AluSc, AluSz, AluSx4.
  • Broad parental names collect almost no firm copies of their own: AluY, AluSx.
  • Near-twin consensuses split the votes, even for a lineage known to be real (AluYb8 and AluYb9, one base apart).
  • Older copies are less often firm with the RepeatMasker bank, and more often firm with the COSEG bank.
  • COSEG and RepeatMasker agree where both are firm, but they are firm on mostly different copies: 11,976 shared, against 106,193 and 54,205.

Neither partition is confirmed by step 2 as a whole, and neither is rejected as a whole. Each recovers a set of subfamilies firmly. Each also contains the same two weak spots: parental consensuses (AluSx, AluY, coseg_sf0), and close consensuses that split their votes (AluYb8 and AluYb9, one base apart; COSEG sf2, sf15 and sf24, which are AluSp and AluSp plus one or two bases).

Not verified

  • The reading of “sequential peeling” above is mine.
  • What the CAS and ASR names (115 and 80 rows) are has not been looked up.
  • Full-length is defined by coverage of the RepeatMasker consensus, so it inherits RepeatMasker's own names and consensus lengths.
  • Several papers were confirmed by title page only (Bennett 2008, Jurka and Smith 1988, Roy 2000, Kojima 2010, Batzer 1996, Batzer and Deininger 2002); Cordaux 2004 was not opened.
  • The online supplement of Price 2004 could not be retrieved; the 213 consensuses exist locally from another copy.
  • The COSEG bank uses consensuses I built from member copies, not COSEG's own (which it did not write), so it may differ slightly from what COSEG would give.
  • The AluYb8/AluYb9 split is checked; whether the same one-base closeness explains the low firm rates of AluSg, AluSc8 and AluSg4 has not been checked.
